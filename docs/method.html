Method

MRI is a computer rating system for college football. It ran as an Excel workbook from 2000 to 2019 and was rebuilt in 2026. Two ratings are published: the original formula, unchanged, and its replacement.

MRI Classic

The 2018 formula, frozen. Each game earns a team credit weighted by how good its opponent was:

win   →  min( 35, margin) × OppWin%  × OppOppWin%
loss  →  max(−35, margin) × OppLoss% × OppOppLoss%

and the season rating adds those up alongside win percentage and four statistical z-scores:

MRI = 25 × Win%  +  25 × OppWin%  +  10 × OppOppWin%  +  Σ game credit
    +  5 × z(RushYds/G)  +  5 × z(PassYds/G)
    +  7 × −z(YdsAllowed/G)  +  3 × z(TurnoverMargin)

The Python port reproduces every rating published across the 2003–2019 workbooks to within 5×10−13, with identical top-25 ordering in all seventeen seasons. It is kept for continuity, and because a baseline you cannot beat is a baseline worth keeping.

MRI 2.0

Classic measures schedule strength as opponents' win percentage times their opponents' win percentage. That treats a 9–3 Sun Belt team and a 9–3 SEC team as equal tests, because win percentage does not know who anyone played.

MRI 2.0 turns every game into one equation —

margin  =  rating(home)  −  rating(away)  +  home field

— and solves the whole season at once. A team's rating then depends on its opponents' ratings, which depend on theirs, all the way down. Strength of schedule stops being a separate statistic and becomes a property of the solution.

Four further differences:

  • Margin is compressed, not capped. Classic truncates at ±35, so a 34-point win and a 60-point win score the same. Here margin passes through a curve that is near-identity inside two touchdowns and flattens beyond it.
  • Ratings are pulled toward a prior carried from last season, so the first month is signal rather than noise. The pull fades on its own as games accumulate.
  • Home field is estimated from the games themselves rather than assumed, and neutral sites are excluded. It currently sits at 3.2 points.
  • FCS opponents are rated individually. Classic pooled every non-FBS team into a single 7–105 entity, so beating North Dakota State and beating Mercer scored identically.

Two numbers

Power answers how good a team is, in points against an average FBS team, so the gap between two teams is a predicted spread. Résumé answers what a team has earned, measured as wins above what an average team would have managed against the same schedule at the same sites. Classic conflated these, which is why it was hard to bet with.

Preseason priors

Every season starts from a prior, and for a long time the prior was last season's rating pulled 30% toward average for everyone. That treats a team that returns its whole roster like one that lost it to the transfer portal. It is now a regression of a season's final rating on three things known before a game is played:

last season's rating, counted at 0.47; the roster's talent (the 247Sports composite, as a z-score within FBS), worth +3.8 points per standard deviation; and returning production (the share of last year's production, by predicted points added, still on the roster), worth +7.9 points from none to all of it.

Fitted on 2015-2025, 2020 excluded (1,297 team-seasons). Scored by leaving each season out in turn it misses by 8.4 points where the old prior missed by 9.1. For the teams that returned under 10% of their production (21 team-seasons) the old prior over-rated them by 8.6 points on average, because a team like that falls about that far from last season. The new prior still over-rates them by about 3.

Exceptions, on purpose. The service academies' recruits are not ranked the way everyone else's are, and the composite calls Army, Navy and Air Force about 2.4 standard deviations worse than average; across 28 team-seasons they beat what that implied by 20 points on average. With talent treated as unmeasured for them the prior is off by 1.8. Teams that were not FBS last year have no comparable last season and keep the old prior.

Talent and results. Talent alone explains about 32% of the variation in a season's rating. Each team page shows what its roster's talent alone would predict next to what it is actually rated. The gap is not just noise: a team that beat its talent one year did so the next with a correlation of 0.46. In testing, most of the improvement came from talent; returning production adds a smaller one on average, and matters most for the few teams that lose nearly everyone.

Home field by stadium

The model gives every home team the same 3.2 points. Tested in September 2026: should that number move with the stadium, or with the trip? Every FBS game from 2013 was predicted from ratings that had only seen earlier weeks, and what the scoreboard said beyond that prediction was split two ways — into the trip (altitude, miles, time zones, early kickoffs, how full the stands usually are), fitted by least squares, and into each stadium, with each stadium's average shrunk toward zero by empirical Bayes: n / (n + k) of its raw mean, where k is game-to-game noise over the real spread between stadiums.

Fitted only on seasons before the one being scored, neither piece, nor both, predicted games better than the single number: the best variant cleared a paired t of +0.17 against a bar of 2, and was better in 4 of 10 seasons. No stadium's own effect can be told apart from average. So the lines are unchanged, and the findings are published as a description of the past. The FCS games are left out of the fit: the model under-rates the FBS side in them by enough to swamp everything else, which is its own problem.

Simulation and the playoff

The simulation page plays the rest of the season 10,000 times. Each run first draws every team's true strength around its rating, with a spread that shrinks as games are played, then plays the remaining games, settles conference standings, plays the ten conference championship games, ranks the teams the way the selection committee would, selects the 12-team field, and plays the bracket. Counting the runs gives each chance.

The committee is modelled, not asked. It ranks by résumé far more than by strength. A blend of 70% résumé and 30% power recovers 10.8 of the committee's true top 12 and 3.7 of its top 4 across 2014–2025, with the average team landing 1.5 places from its real rank. A little noise is added on top, because even at its best the blend misses about one team in twelve. The field follows this year's rules: the ACC, Big 12, Big Ten and SEC champions are in; so is the highest-ranked team from the American, Conference USA, MAC, Mountain West, Pac-12 and Sun Belt; Notre Dame is in if it is ranked in the top 12; the remaining places go to the highest-ranked teams left.

Simplifications, stated. Ties in conference wins are broken by coin flip, not head-to-head. Injuries are invisible until they show up in results. The committee blend was chosen using the same twelve seasons it is reported on.

Who to root for

Each team page lists the games this week that the team is not playing in but that move its playoff odds most, and the side to cheer. For every game still to kick off, the simulation is run twice more on the same random numbers: once with the home side forced to win, once with the visitor. The difference in a team’s odds between the two is that game’s effect on it. Using the same random numbers matters: both runs share every draw except that one game’s, so what is left is the game and not simulation noise. Reading the effect off the ordinary run instead — comparing the runs where the underdog happened to win with the rest — would mix in something else, because those are also the runs in which the underdog was drawn stronger for the rest of its season. A forced upset is drawn the way real upsets happen, usually close, not as a blowout.

The page shows at most five games, ranked by how much they move the playoff odds; anything under half a point is left out as noise. When nothing moves a team’s playoff odds that much but a game moves its conference title odds, the guide switches to those and says so. A team with less than a half-percent playoff chance, or more than 99.5%, gets one line instead: nothing this weekend moves the needle, or its fate is in its own hands.

What it cannot do

A margin error near 13 points is roughly where closing betting spreads sit. That is the honest signal that this model should not be expected to beat a closing line. If it has an edge anywhere it is against opening numbers, and that claim will be backtested and reported here before it is acted on — including if the answer is no.
